The work is already on your desk. A reply is waiting in the inbox. A booking still needs a time. A post is unwritten. A meeting has finished, and the next steps were never written down. The rest of the inbox is still unsorted. An invoice is overdue. Someone wants a simple report from numbers you already have.

Those are the seven tasks below: a customer email, a set of meeting times, a short social post, meeting notes, an inbox priority list, an invoice message, and a first pass at a report. A tool can prepare each one. A named person still checks the facts and decides what is sent.

[How to Start Using AI in Your Small Business Without Losing Human Control](/blog/how-to-start-using-ai-in-your-small-business-without-losing-human-control) already sets the pattern: one job, one named person, and a way to stop. This article stays with that pattern. It follows what the publishers’ own pages say those tools prepare.

Each illustration below is fictional. It shows the check. It is not a customer story, and it is not a result. A button or a plan on your own account can differ from the help page. If the account in front of you does not show the feature, leave it unused.

## What the tool prepares, and three steps that come after you act

Preparation means the tool drafts, summarises, or proposes. A person sends, publishes, approves, or discards.

A booking stays a proposal until you send the times. A reminder stays unsent until you approve it. You still name the person who may send or publish.

## If the business is in Australia

Read the country, language, and plan limits as part of the task.

Google describes “Help me write” on a personal Gmail account as restricted to the US. Eligible Workspace or Google AI plans are described as available in multiple supported languages globally.

Treat a US-only, beta, or plan-gated feature as unavailable until your own account shows it. This article does not describe Australian law or tax rules.

## 1. Customer email drafting

A customer is waiting, and the reply is still blank. The tool can write a first draft. You still send it.

**What the tool can prepare.** Microsoft’s Outlook help says Copilot can draft a message from a prompt. That path is limited to specified Outlook versions, and New Outlook and Outlook on the web do not draft plain-text messages this way.

In Gmail, Google’s help for “Help me write” says Gemini can generate a new draft or refine text you already have. A specific prompt, including a named email or file, is how the page says you aim for factual accuracy, and the same page says the draft can also pull details from your other emails and Drive files, such as flight times or booking codes, without an explicit instruction to use that file. Google says a draft may be inaccurate or inappropriate, and that Gemini features should not be relied on as financial, legal, or other professional advice.

Google’s Workspace Privacy Hub, for qualifying Workspace editions, says Gemini interactions stay in the organisation. Gemini does not use that content to train generative models outside the domain without permission, Gemini can access Workspace content the person is already allowed to access, prompt retention on that hub is admin-controlled from 90 days to indefinite, and those Workspace statements were not on the personal-Gmail portion of the drafting page. Keep them with qualifying Workspace editions.

**Fictional illustration.** A sole trader asks for a short, polite reply explaining that Thursday’s visit has moved to Friday and that the quoted price is unchanged. Before sending, they check the customer’s name, the day, and the price against their own booking note.

**What you check.** Confirm every fact the draft states. Decide whether the tone is acceptable. You are the person who sends it. If Sources shows an unrelated email or file, remove that detail.

**Limits.** If a draft pulls in another email or a Drive file, remove that detail before you send. If the account does not show the draft feature, leave it unused.

**First step.** Draft one reply in the email tool the business already uses. Compare it with the source email before anyone sends it.

## 2. Appointment scheduling

A reply often leads to the next question: when can we meet?

**What the tool can prepare.** Google’s Gmail page for “Help me schedule” requires an eligible Workspace or Google AI plan, and from a draft or a reply Gemini proposes times when you and your guests are available. The page states a maximum of 20 guests and four proposed slots by default. The guest list does not update if recipients change after the card is inserted, a green slot means all guests are available, an amber slot is a conflict you select manually, availability checks are limited to calendars you can access, and suggestions may be inaccurate and are not professional advice. The same page says that booking creates the calendar event and invites the guests, and that a booking page from an appointment schedule you already have is an option on that page.

You already chose which times to send. You still decide whether that meeting goes ahead.

Microsoft’s Outlook page says Copilot chat can suggest available times, that Copilot checks attendee calendars and can recommend a mutually convenient time, and that the page does not state a separate limit on how many people this chat scheduling supports, so none is stated here.

**Fictional illustration.** A consultant drafting a reply to two clients clicks Help me schedule, sees one amber slot, drops that slot, and sends the remaining times. The clients’ choice creates the invite.

**What you check.** Check the guests, the duration, and the time zone. Do not send an amber slot as if everyone were free. You send the proposal yourself.

**Limits.** A suggestion can be wrong. The feature also sits behind an eligible plan. If your account does not show it, treat it as unavailable.

**First step.** Propose times for one real meeting, review the colour of each slot, and send only the slots you have checked.

## 3. Social media content preparation

Once the practical arrangements are done, many businesses still need a few public words. A post is a draft until a person publishes it.

**What the tool can prepare.** Google’s prompt help for Gemini in Docs, Sheets, Slides, Vids, and Forms says the samples are illustrative; an eligible Workspace or Google AI plan is required. Google’s Workspace guide, “Use Gemini for marketing,” says you can use the feature only if your organisation supports it, and that suggestions may be inaccurate and should not be treated as professional advice.

The words are the preparation. You publish them.

**Fictional illustration.** A café owner pastes a short Docs prompt, names this week’s soup and the street address, rejects a draft that adds a discount they are not offering, and posts the checked text themselves.

**What you check.** Check prices, dates, claims, and the call to action. Revise a vague prompt before publication. You publish the post. If the post includes a photorealistic picture, video, or realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created or altered, use the platform’s disclosure tool.

**Limits.** A caption can invent an offer.

**First step.** Draft three short posts in a document. Publish none until a person has checked every concrete claim.

## 4. Meeting summaries

A meeting can go well and still leave you with nothing written down. Notes are useful. They are also easy to trust too quickly.

**What the tool can prepare.** Microsoft’s short Teams recap page says Copilot can say what you missed, including whether you were mentioned and any action items assigned to you. The same page says to verify the results, because AI-generated content could be incorrect, and that page does not describe a recording-consent rule.

Google Meet’s “Take notes for me” requires an eligible Workspace edition or a Google AI plan. “Take notes for me” is documented in English, French, German, Italian, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese, and Spanish, one spoken language at a time, and it can put the notes in a Google Doc, offer “Summary so far” if someone joins late, and email the organiser a recap link. The organiser can limit recipients to hosts and co-hosts, to people in the organisation, or to all invited guests, including people outside the organisation, invited guests are the people on the calendar invite, not only the people who attended, and notes are saved in the organiser’s Drive and are subject to the organisation’s Meet retention policy. If the Google Account is through work or school, an administrator may turn “Take notes for me” on automatically for some meetings, participants are told that notes are being taken, an administrator may require explicit consent before note-taking, recording, or transcription, and that control is off by default. The same “Take notes for me” page says a summary can be incomplete, inaccurate, or missing, including when the meeting is shorter than the recommended 15 minutes, notes are recommended for meetings from 15 minutes to 8 hours, and if screenshots are included a presentation must be on screen for at least 10 seconds, and participants see a message that Gemini may add screenshots.

You choose who receives those notes before the conversation turns confidential.

**Fictional illustration.** Two business partners turn notes on at the start of a supplier call, tell the supplier, and afterwards compare the “next steps” with what they remember before anyone emails the supplier.

**What you check.** Someone who was in the meeting accepts, edits, or discards each action item and each decision. The host chooses who receives the document before a confidential discussion starts. Anyone allowed to stop notes can stop them for a sensitive stretch. On Meet, host controls can limit that power to hosts and co-hosts.

**Limits.** A wrong action item can be emailed to invited people who were not in the room. Notes and screenshots are copies of the meeting. If your edition or plan does not show Meet notes, treat them as unavailable.

**First step.** Use notes for one internal meeting. Keep the summary inside the business until a participant has checked it.

## 5. Customer enquiry triage

Writing one reply is one job. Seeing which messages need you first is another. Use the view, then open the message.

**What the tool can prepare.** Google’s page for Gmail AI Inbox says the feature is in beta and may change, and that it is available only in the US and in English, for an eligible Google AI plan on a personal account or an eligible Workspace plan for work. Suggested to-dos are high-priority items that need attention, and topics to catch up on are short summaries of updates spread across conversations. The view is based on the Primary tab and does not include Social, Promotions, spam, or mail you archive, delete, mute, or snooze, and it does not support attachments, delegated inboxes, or encrypted email. Smart features must be on, the first to-dos and topics may take up to 7 days to appear, and it is not documented as a system that classifies customer complaints or chooses a refund.

You can open the related email, reply, mark a to-do done, or remove it.

Use it as a view of mail, then open the message.

**Fictional illustration.** A shop owner opens AI Inbox, sees a suggested to-do from a customer asking about an order, opens the source email, and only then decides whether to reply.

**What you check.** Open the underlying message before you act. You decide what is urgent. A missing item is not proof that no customer wrote.

**Limits.** The view can omit mail outside Primary, mail with attachments, and encrypted mail. A priority label is a model judgement on a beta feature. A summary can omit or misstate the request. A short priority list can sound more certain than the mail behind it. If you are outside the US, or your account is not in English, treat AI Inbox as unavailable until the account shows it.

**First step.** Turn the view on, wait until it has had time to populate, and for one day compare its to-dos with the inbox before you rely on it.

## 6. Invoice and payment reminder preparation

Some of those messages are about money that has not arrived. A reminder is still a financial message. Match it to the invoice before anyone approves it.

**What the tool can prepare.** Intuit’s QuickBooks page, “Get started with the Payments AI,” says Payments AI uses payment history to suggest payment settings and communications. The page describes it for US customers on Essentials, Plus, Advanced, and Intuit Enterprise Suite, and only for users who can edit or create invoices. It can remind customers about invoices unpaid by the due date, suggest a reminder schedule you review before you approve, and write an editable message that includes the invoice number, the customer name, and the due date, and automatic invoice reminders can be turned off. Payments AI suggests a late fee that you can apply or change, it drafts an invoice email and a subject line, a default subject line or email body is shown first, and the AI-generated subject and body are optional suggestions. Auto-fill can extract information from a document, a photo, or text into an invoice, and the page says you might be asked to review and double-check some of that data.

**Fictional illustration.** A US QuickBooks user opens an overdue invoice, reads the suggested reminder, corrects the amount if it does not match the invoice, and only then approves the schedule.

**What you check.** Amounts, invoice numbers, due dates, and recipients come from the accounting record, not from the sentence the model wrote. You approve the reminder schedule. You can apply or change a suggested late fee. You send or approve the invoice.

**Limits.** An editable message can still contain the wrong figure if you do not compare it with the invoice. Auto-fill can extract the wrong field from a photo or an email. This is financial communication. If your account does not show the feature, treat it as unavailable.

**First step.** Prepare one reminder or one invoice draft. Match the customer, the amount, and the due date to the accounting record before you approve it.

## 7. Business reporting

The last task is a question about figures you already keep. Ask it of a clean table, then check the answer yourself.

**What the tool can prepare.** Microsoft’s Excel help says Copilot can answer questions about data in one or more tables, or in ranges laid out like tables. Unstructured data is not supported, and Copilot does not modify the workbook. It may return a static table or chart you can insert, those inserts are not refreshable, and advanced analysis mode on the same page opens a new sheet and can insert refreshable Python.

Google’s Workspace Privacy Hub says “analyzing data in a Google Sheet” is an example of a personalised Gemini response, and a detailed Sheets feature page is not part of this evidence, so this article does not describe Sheets charts or formulas.

**Fictional illustration.** An owner with last quarter’s sales in an Excel table asks which service brought in the most revenue, then checks the answer against the total column before mentioning it to anyone else.

**What you check.** Check the answer against the source rows. Ask what period and which columns were used. Keep the sentence out of a bank, tax, or board report until it matches the sheet.

**Limits.** A confident summary can still mis-count. A static chart does not update when the sheet changes. The code view on Microsoft’s Excel page is a way to inspect the calculation, including an optional summary of that code. You still check the result.

**First step.** Ask one question of a clean table. Compare the answer with a total you calculate yourself.

## One draft, one person, then stop

Choose one of these seven tasks that you already do in a tool your account already shows.

A customer email is a practical place to begin when that draft feature is already there. Take one reply you still owe. Ask for the draft. Leave it unsent. Name the person who may send it, and write their name on the draft. Stop there. That person checks the customer’s name, the day, and any price or promise against your own record. Then they send it, or they discard it.

The same stop fits the other six tasks: a set of meeting times, a short post, meeting notes, the inbox view, an invoice message, or a question about a table. One task. One named person. Nothing is sent or published until that person has checked it.
