## Speed is not the same as stewardship

AI drafts can save time. They can also multiply mistakes if you publish them unchecked. The point is not to reject AI. The point is to keep responsibility where it belongs: with the person or ministry whose name goes on the work.

According to NIST, the AI Risk Management Framework is intended for voluntary use and to improve the ability to incorporate trustworthiness considerations into the design, development, use, and evaluation of AI products, services, and systems.

According to NIST, AI risk management should be integrated into broader enterprise risk processes.

That is a useful posture for creators too. Trustworthiness is not optional polish. It belongs in the workflow before publish.

According to OpenAI usage policies, responsible use is a shared priority, and those policies are no substitute for legal requirements, professional duties, or ethical obligations that should influence how people use AI. In plain terms: the tool does not excuse the publisher.

## A 6-step pre-publish fact-check habit

### Step 1: Identify every claim that sounds like a fact

Read the draft once and highlight:

- Names of people, organizations, or products
- Dates, numbers, and "research shows" statements
- Instructions that sound like professional advice
- Absolute language: always, never, guaranteed, proven

If you skip this step, confident prose will hide what needs checking.

### Step 2: Separate facts from interpretations

Turn one paragraph into two columns:

- Column A: What is being stated as fact
- Column B: What is interpretation, opinion, or pastoral application

Interpretations can be rewritten for clarity. Facts must be verified or removed.

### Step 3: Verify the facts you plan to keep

Use primary sources where you can. According to the IFCN Code of Principles, signatories want their readers to be able to verify findings themselves and provide all sources in enough detail that readers can replicate their work. That standard is a good model for ministries and creators, even when you are not an IFCN signatory.

Practical rule:

- If you cannot verify a claim quickly and confidently, remove it, soften it, or rewrite it as experience rather than authority.

### Step 4: Replace false certainty with honest language

Examples:

- "This will fix" becomes "This can help."
- "Experts agree" becomes a named source, or it is deleted.
- "The law requires" becomes a verified citation, or it is deleted.

### Step 5: Make responsibility visible

Add a short note when needed:

- what the content is
- what it is not
- that readers should verify details for their own situation

### Step 6: Run an ethical risk check before you schedule

Ask:

- Could this mislead someone?
- Could this push someone toward harm?
- Could this damage trust if wrong?
- Does this match our values and mission?

## Boundary checks that belong in the same habit

According to OpenAI usage policies, people may not use its services for attempts to infringe on intellectual property rights of others.

Those boundaries belong in a pre-publish review:

1. **IP cleanliness:** rewrite anything that feels borrowed, branded, or proprietary.
2. **Advice boundary:** keep educational content educational; do not present AI output as personalized professional counsel.
3. **Audience care:** ask who is likely to see the piece, not only who you intended.

## A ministry-focused example

If an AI draft says, "This practice is guaranteed to change your life," or "This method is biblically required," treat that as a red flag. Encouragement can remain strong without false certainty.

Safer:

- "Here is a practice that has helped many believers build consistency."
- "Here is one responsible way churches implement this."

## Why this matters

Publishing is stewardship. Once something is public, people share it, teach it, and build decisions on it. Fact-checking an AI draft is one concrete way to practise responsible use. You are protecting people and preserving credibility.
